Tips for better results

  • Use top-down (nadir) RGB imagery with natural colours. Oblique photos and infrared false-colour images are not suitable.
  • Higher-resolution imagery (≈ 0.1–1 m per pixel) shows the mixed colours and texture that dumping sites produce.
  • Dense villages, quarries and rocky ground also look "busy" and can produce false candidates. Always verify on site.
  • Adjust the Detection Settings panel and press Reanalyse Image to compare different sensitivities.

1

Image upload

You choose or drop a JPG, PNG or WEBP aerial / satellite image. The file is read by your browser only; nothing is uploaded.

2

Pre-processing

The image is decoded, resized to the chosen analysis resolution (aspect ratio preserved) and its RGB values are extracted from an HTML canvas.

3

Land-cover classification

Every pixel is assigned to vegetation, built-up, water or barren land using colour-space rules (excess-green index, hue, saturation, brightness and local smoothness).

4

Potential waste identification

Local colour variation, texture, bright/dark mixing, colour diversity and edge irregularity are combined into a waste-likelihood score for each pixel.

5

Region grouping

Pixels above the sensitivity threshold are cleaned with morphological filters and grouped into connected regions; tiny and thread-like regions are discarded.

6

Detection overlay

Suspected waste is painted red on top of the original image; other classes use green, blue, grey and yellow. The opacity is adjustable.

7

Statistical analysis

Pixel counts and percentages are computed for the five classes, and every candidate region receives an area, a score and a review priority.

8

Report generation

A PDF report, CSV table and detection image are created locally. The analysis is also stored in the browser history.

What the colours mean

1. Approach

WasteScan is a rule-based (heuristic) image-processing system. It does not use a neural network, a trained model, a satellite API or any online service. All numbers are computed from the pixels of the image you provide, in JavaScript, on your computer.

2. Pre-processing

The image is drawn onto an HTML canvas at the selected analysis resolution (long side 480 / 800 / 1200 px) with high-quality smoothing and its RGBA buffer is read. Large images are therefore down-sampled before analysis. Processing runs in a Web Worker to keep the interface responsive.

3. Per-pixel features

  • Colour spaces – RGB, HSV and luminance (0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B).
  • Excess-green index ExG = (2G − R − B) / (R + G + B) for vegetation.
  • Colour buckets – each pixel is quantised into one of 8 colour families (dark, bright, grey, red, orange/brown, green, blue, purple).

4. Neighbourhood (window) features

Using separable box filters over a (2r+1)² window (r grows with image size) the following are computed for every pixel:

  • RGB colour variation – √(σR² + σG² + σB²) within the window.
  • Texture – local standard deviation of luminance.
  • Edge density – mean Sobel gradient magnitude.
  • Edge irregularity – edge density multiplied by (1 − orientation coherence). Orientation coherence uses the fourth power of the complex gradient, so straight edges and rectangular building grids score as regular and chaotic debris scores as irregular.
  • Colour diversity – Shannon entropy of the 8-bucket colour histogram.
  • Bright + dark mixing – min(share of dark pixels, share of bright pixels).
  • Local contrast – distance between the local mean colour and the mean of a 3× larger surrounding window (distinct from surrounding vegetation/land).
  • Synthetic colours – share of strongly saturated blue-to-magenta pixels (tarps, plastics).

5. Waste-likelihood score

Each cue is normalised to 0–1 and combined with fixed weights (colour variation 0.20, diversity 0.18, irregularity 0.15, contrast 0.15, bright/dark mixing 0.13, texture 0.11, synthetic colour 0.08). The score is reduced where the context is unlikely to be waste: calm water, flat surfaces and homogeneous green canopy. Pixels whose local colour variation is below the colour variation threshold are excluded outright.

6. Region grouping

The sensitivity slider sets the score threshold (threshold = 0.70 − 0.45 × sensitivity/100). The binary mask is cleaned by morphological opening (removes thin lines such as field edges and river banks) and closing (joins nearby fragments). Connected components are then labelled with an 8-neighbour flood fill. Regions smaller than the minimum region size, or long and thin (major/minor axis ratio above 3.5), are discarded.

7. Land-cover classification

Water: smooth, blue/teal dominant (or dark and bluish). Vegetation: positive excess-green with a green hue. Barren land: warm brown/tan tones. Built-up: neutral grey/white and strongly coloured roofs. A 5×5 majority filter removes isolated pixels. Pixels inside accepted regions become Waste Dumping (suspected).

8. Detection score and priority

A region's score is 100 × (0.7 × mean pixel score + 0.3 × peak pixel score). Scores ≥ 70 are labelled High priority, ≥ 58 Medium and the rest Low. The label ranks candidates for field verification only.

9. Reliability checks

The system warns when an image is tiny, almost grayscale, extremely dark or bright, very blurry, nearly uniform, or when more than 35% of it is flagged – situations where RGB heuristics cannot be trusted.

10. Limitations

  • No machine-learning model and no ground-truth validation: accuracy is unknown on real imagery.
  • Dense settlements, quarries, rocky ground, plastic-covered farmland and shadows can be mistaken for waste; clean-looking waste can be missed.
  • Only visible RGB is used – no infrared, thermal or spectral-index data.
  • Region areas are in analysis-resolution pixels; conversion to hectares needs the ground sampling distance you provide.
Project title

Identification of Illegal Waste Dumping Sites Using Remote Sensing and Image Analysis

Website name: WasteScan  ·  Tagline: Detect. Analyse. Protect.

Problem statement

Illegal waste dumping creates environmental pollution, affects public health, and damages natural ecosystems. Traditional manual monitoring is time-consuming and requires significant human effort.

Proposed solution

WasteScan provides a browser-based image analysis platform that examines aerial and satellite images to identify potential waste dumping locations.

Technology used

Remote SensingAerial Image AnalysisRGB Colour AnalysisDigital Image Processing Pixel ClassificationRegion DetectionHTMLCSSJavaScriptHTML Canvas

Study area

Satara–Songav region, Maharashtra, India.

The current prototype uses image-based RGB analysis and does not depend on physical sensors.

Important notes

  • WasteScan uses local image processing and heuristic classification. It does not use a trained deep-learning model, a neural network or a satellite API.
  • Results are estimates and are not scientifically validated waste detection. Detected regions are candidates only and need verification by a person.
  • Everything runs offline in your browser. Images are never uploaded, and analysis history is stored only in this browser's localStorage.
